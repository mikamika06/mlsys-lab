Specifically, our analysis tooling is failing to locate the precise losing batch size—defined as the minimum batch size that violates our maximum allowed latency constraint or experiences severe queueing degradation. Second, when tuning the maximum number of concurrent sequences (`max_num_seqs`) under a strict Time-to-First-Token Service Level Objective, our scheduling simulator miscalculates the capacity threshold. As the prefill queue grows, the time to process prefill tokens spikes due to compute saturation and memory bandwidth contention. The optimization routine fails to accurately determine the optimal `max_num_seqs` limit from an array of measured latencies, resulting in either excessive queuing delays or overly conservative concurrency caps that degrade overall system throughput.

Third, our automated benchmarking harness frequently falls victim to artifactual warm-up noise. During initial iterations of execution, CUDA kernel launches, JIT compilation effects, memory allocator initialization, and cache coldness introduce transient latency spikes. Because our benchmarking script fails to correctly spot and discard these missing-warmup artifacts, our median and tail latency metrics become polluted with outlier values from the cold-start phase. This leads to erroneous hardware performance characterization, false regressions in CI, and incorrect scaling projections across different quantization levels.
